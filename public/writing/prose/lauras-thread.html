Laura’s Thread —
Laura had discovered knitting by accident. One rainy afternoon she wandered into the local craft store, drawn by the warm light and the soft colours stacked on the shelves. The women inside welcomed her instantly, guiding her hands through the first clumsy stitches. Soon she was visiting every week, buying yarns she didn’t need, bobbins she didn’t understand, and needles in every size. Knitting became her refuge — a quiet, repetitive rhythm that drowned out the noise of her life.Her marriage had been unraveling for years. Her husband drifted from job to job, always tired, always complaining, always collapsing onto the sofa with the weight of a man who expected the world to carry him. Laura had carried him. She had carried him far too long.The betrayal with her sister had been the final tear. She had discovered it in a way that felt almost staged: a message left open, a glance exchanged, a silence too heavy to ignore. Her sister had not denied it. Her husband had barely apologised. Laura had not spoken to her sister since.



Now they were all travelling together — her husband, her sister, her new partner Kathy, and Laura herself — on a three‑hour drive to La Rochelle to visit her grandmother. Laura sat in the back seat, knitting the first jumper she had ever attempted. The yarn was soft, pale blue, the colour of calm seas. She made sure her seatbelt was firmly clicked in. She didn’t trust her husband’s driving, and she trusted her sister even less.



But Laura had prepared for this trip.A week earlier, she had noticed the car door seals were worn. She patched them quietly, carefully, making sure no one saw. She siphoned off the brake fluid and stored it in the house, telling herself it was temporary, telling herself she was only being cautious. She knew about the odour it left behind. She poured the brake fluid into the back of the car just before they left, her hands steady, her breath calm.She told herself she wasn’t planning anything. She told herself she was only thinking.The motorway stretched ahead in a long grey ribbon. Her husband talked loudly, gesturing at nothing. Her sister laughed at something



Kathy whispered. Laura kept knitting, the needles clicking softly, a heartbeat she controlled.Then the lorry in front of them braked.Her husband reacted too slowly.



He had been talking, distracted. The car slammed forward, metal folding like cloth. Laura felt her knitting needles jerk in her hands; she grabbed the gear lever as the impact threw her forward. The seat in front of her pierced her husband’s side. Time slowed, then collapsed into silence.Emergency services arrived. They cut them from the wreckage. Laura drifted in and out of consciousness, the world a blur of lights and voices.She woke in a hospital room, surrounded by friends and family. The doctors told her she had been lucky. Her husband and her sister had not survived.The police visited her soon after. A woman officer asked gentle questions. Laura could barely remember anything. The officer mentioned the knitting needles — how they had gone straight through the car seat behind her. Laura shivered. The officer reassured her that she wasn’t responsible, that she should focus on healing.But Laura remembered the brake fluid.
She remembered the patched seals.
She remembered the odour.She cried for a long time after the police left.In the quiet of the hospital room, she stared at the ceiling. The red glow of the emergency exit sign painted the walls in a strange, warm hue. She remembered losing the sharp needles. She remembered her husband’s voice telling her once, half‑joking, that she’d kill him someday with her “ridiculous hobbies.”Knitting wasn’t just for grandmothers. It was an innocent habit, a craft she had cultivated carefully. And now, with the insurance payout and the sudden quiet of her life, she realised she had crafted something else entirely: a future without the people who had betrayed her.But the story didn’t end there.A week after she returned home, Laura found a letter slipped under her door. No stamp. No name. Just a single sentence written in a familiar hand:“You didn’t think I’d leave you alone, did you?”Her sister’s handwriting.Laura stared at the letter, her breath tightening.
She had seen her sister’s body.
She had attended the funeral.
She had watched the coffin lower into the earth.Yet the handwriting was unmistakable.The next day, a second letter arrived.
Then a third.



Each one more unsettling than the last.Someone knew what she had done.
Someone knew about the brake fluid.
Someone knew about the patched seals.And someone was coming.Laura picked up her knitting needles — the new pair she had bought after the accident. The metal glinted in the lamplight. Her hands were steady.



She had crafted her freedom once.She could do it again.
