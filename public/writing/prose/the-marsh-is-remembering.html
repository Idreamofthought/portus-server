The Marsh Is Remembering

2 October 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026 — Richard Jenkins · Fiction · Halloween

1. Alice’s Story · 2. The Landlord’s Story · 3. The Boatman’s Story · 4. Elin’s Story · The Marsh Aftermath

’Twould be hard to find a grimmer place than the Medway marshes, even today. Thick mud sucked at every misplaced step, and the air carried a smell fit to raise the very dead. In autumn, a chill swept up from the ground. Those abroad at dawn hoped the sun would burn their fears away; those caught at dusk watched the last silhouettes dissolve into a foggy ocean.

Such evenings seemed to swallow sound, light, even time.

Yet the folk who lived beside them feared the marsh for more than its mud and stench. They said that, after sunset, the fog went looking for travellers—and that those who heard their names whispered from the reeds were never seen again.

There were dangers enough with names and faces. Around the next bend might wait a highwayman, a smuggler, a fellow whose interest in your journey began and ended with your purse. The gangs took their cut. You paid—or took a cut yourself.

Smugglers had good reason to encourage tales of things moving in the marshes. A frightened witness stayed indoors. A haunted landing remained conveniently empty.

Many a traveller would splutter past the churchyards, with their stone pillows and their promises of “Here lies” and “late of this parish”, hoping not to find themselves tucked into that particular cold bed.

How gladly, then, they welcomed the lights of an inn.

Chapter 1 — Alice’s Story

Alice had been in her little shop for over thirty years. She would make potions and medicines, pastilles, tinctures, even just sloe gin for the evenings or peppermint cordial, using plants steeped in alcohol. She knew that if you rubbed some plants onto wounds they would heal faster, or the pain would go. As she sat in her kitchen, she wondered what would have happened to her a hundred or two hundred years ago, when she would probably have been accused of witchcraft and burnt at the stake.

She ordered everyone around. She played her part in the community, bringing the babes into the world and laying out the dead, and sometimes, tragically, both on the same day. She’d seen people come with last-chance looks in their eyes, with a nagging doubt or just a wee problem that needed an everyday solution, like constipation or charming warts.

She knew her customer was coming. He’d been before. His wife was sick again with the ague from the swamps and the marshes. There wasn’t much to be done. She could give something to lift the spirits, maybe some ginger, maybe a bit of bark from a willow to cool the fever, and she would add a little laudanum, perhaps, to help the patient.

Chapter 2 — The Landlord’s Story

Daniel was from Ireland. He had come to England as a young man and served in the army, spending part of his time among the Medway forts. Later, when he was out of the army, he found work in a small tavern where he could learn the trade.

Here he learnt to tend the ale, even brew his own beer, change the barrels and cook a meal. He met his future wife too. They married and sought out an inn of their own, settling into the Three Mariners in Lower Rainham around 1830.

They had children, and business was good. Travellers and locals frequented the place, men who worked on the water or in the fields, people who wanted a meal, a drink or a little company before going home.

His wife cooked, his daughter served, his son too. Daniel kept a cudgel behind the bar. Most days it stayed there, but there were drunkards enough, and now and then someone needed reminding whose house they were in.

The landlord was a jolly fellow, stout in body and hearty in manner, with a face you were glad to find at the end of a day’s travel. Lanterns hung from iron brackets outside. Through the bull’s-eye windowpanes came a blurred glimpse of firelight, moving shoulders and raised tankards.

Inside, folks warmed their toes in the inglenook while a ham glistened beside the fire. A warm meal awaited those who arrived in good time; the latecomer might settle for cheese and bread, and count himself fortunate.

Around the fireplaces of the inns of the world, many a tall tale was spun. Some were tall, and some were true, and after a third tankard the distinction seldom troubled anyone.

“They found his cart,” said the landlord. “His bundle was still beneath the seat.”

“Then he’d nothing worth stealing,” someone said.

The landlord laughed with the rest and reached for the carving knife.

Chapter 3 — The Boatman’s Story

But the traveller had a name, and it was Thomas Vale. He had left his wife, Elin, sick in a house on the Isle of Grain, beyond the alder trees, across the Medway. Her fever rose each night and left her weaker by morning. The physician had sent him to an apothecary in Rainham for a bottle of medicine. If Thomas returned before dawn, Elin might live.

Before fastening his cloak, Thomas felt beneath his shirt for the little locket. Inside lay a curl of hair, finer than either his or Elin’s. She had cut it herself. Neither of them opened it now.

The physician called her illness an ague. Thomas knew only the shaking, the burning skin, and how small her hand had felt in his that morning.

He held the locket through the cloth.

He had come home too late once before.

Daniel nodded towards a man sitting by the fire.

“Will. This gentleman wants to get across to Grain.”

The boatman looked up, then towards the window. He put on his coat and went outside to see what the fog and the water were doing.

When Will came back into the Three Mariners, the damp was still on his coat. He stood by the fire and warmed his hands.

“We’ll have to wait a bit. The tide’s still going out.”

Thomas looked down at his bag. He had the medicine now, but his wife was over on Grain, sick again with the ague, and he could do nothing for her sitting here.

“How long?”

“Half an hour, perhaps.”

Daniel offered him another ale, but he had hardly finished the first. From the kitchen came the smell of something cooking. His daughter carried a plate past him while his son brought in more wood. They all had things to do. Thomas could only wait.

“My wife’s ill,” he said. “I’ve been to get something for the fever.”

Will nodded.

“We’ll go when we can.”

When at last he reached for his coat, Thomas was already standing. Daniel gave them a lantern and watched them leave.

The fog had crept up from the river. It lay along the road and hid the houses, the hedges, even the ground a little way ahead. Thomas remembered the warmth of the room almost as soon as he was outside. Here there was the cold, the damp and that strange silence.

He kept close to Will as they turned down towards Bloors Wharf. He could hear their boots in the mud. Once he heard water somewhere ahead, though he could see nothing of it.

Will stopped at the boat.

“Give me that while you get in.”

Thomas passed him the bag and stepped down carefully. The boat rocked and he caught at its side.

“Sit yourself down.”

Will handed back the medicine. Thomas held it between his knees as the boatman got in and pushed them away. When his pole no longer found the bottom, he laid it down and took up the oars.

After a few strokes Thomas looked back, but the wharf had already gone into the fog.

He thought of his wife lying at home, sometimes burning hot, sometimes shaking so much he could hardly hold her. Perhaps Alice’s medicine would help. Perhaps by morning the fever would have cooled and she would ask for something to eat.

He held the bag a little closer. Will rowed on.

They crossed in silence. Thomas listened to the dip of the blades until he realised he could no longer hear the water against the hull. Halfway over, he turned his head, as though he had heard Elin calling from behind. The boatman saw only the pale blur of his face, and, for an instant, another pale shape beside it.

Then the fog thickened.

It was no longer mist upon the water, but something that moved against the boat, searching. Cold dampness gathered on Thomas’s hands. Pale shapes passed beyond the gunwales, keeping pace with them beneath the surface. Voices whispered from every side, first too faint to understand, then calling Thomas by name in the voices of the dead.

One voice sounded like Elin.

When it thinned again, Thomas was gone.

The boatman called and searched the water with his pole. He searched beneath the seats and along the bottom boards. There he found the bottle, still stoppered, and Thomas’s bundle, damp with marsh water. The cloth was cold enough to sting his fingers.

He carried both to the far bank and, after asking among the cottages, learned that Thomas’s wife lay ill in a house beyond the alder trees.

No one remembered seeing Thomas arrive.

Chapter 4 — Elin’s Story

She received the boatman in bed, her face white against the pillow. He set the bottle and bundle beside her and told her what little he knew. As he spoke, water ticked steadily from the bundle onto the floor, though the cloth was no longer wet.

At the mention of the fog, she looked towards the open door. Beyond it, the mist had gathered in the yard. It lay between the alder trees, and for a moment she thought she heard Thomas calling her name.

Elin did not die that night.

The boatman had brought her the medicine, and in time the fever passed. She grew strong enough to rise, to tend the fire, to open the door and look beyond the alder trees. But Thomas did not come home.

She waited for him, and for the baby they had lost. Sometimes she thought she heard them when the fog came creeping up from the marshes, a voice she knew, a small cry. She would stand listening until the cold brought her back inside.

There were years still to live. Bread to cut, washing to dry, people who came and sat awhile. Sometimes she laughed, then wished she could tell Thomas what had been so funny.

Her hair softened into silver. Still she waited, bound by mourning and the weight of love. Love that had sent him across the water to find her medicine. Love that saved her, love that cost her.

And love that finally freed her, the day her soft silver hair finally swept into the frothing fog.

The Marsh Aftermath

For years after Thomas vanished, the alder trees stood heavy with fog even on clear nights. The villagers barred their shutters before sunset. Children were kept from the water’s edge. Some said the bundle the boatman brought home still dripped marsh water, though the cloth was dry to the touch.

Some swore they heard knocking from the reeds.

Others said they heard names, spoken in the tone of someone standing very close behind them.

Those who saw shapes in the mist said they looked like men walking home after long journeys—heads bowed, shoulders slumped—but their footprints never led towards the marsh.

Only out of it.

And now, when the fog rises at dusk, the villagers say:

“Close the door. The marsh is remembering.”
