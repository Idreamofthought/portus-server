Inspector Partridge Mysteries: The Lighthouse Murders







Prologue



The call had come from the chief while Partridge was working in Essex.



“We have a murder in Chatham for you. You’re a local man, Partridge, so it’s down to you. You have the background and experience for this job.”



“I can tell you who the victim is, but be aware the parents want no publicity,” the chief added.



Partridge had sighed into the phone. “In a murder case, that’s impossible.”



“You’ll have to get them to play ball, and help them through the nightmare ahead,” retorted the chief.



“The local press won’t play ball,” Partridge insisted.



“So you’ll hold a press conference. The family’s name is Green, and the victim’s father is the local Mayor.”



He had phoned the parents and made an appointment. Bags under his eyes stung; he felt freewheeling toward the end of a long career.



In the living room, husband and wife had sat together, raw with grief. Partridge had offered his condolences and cautioned them: “The publicity will be enormous. TV and press will be down on you like vultures, and the rumour mill will run overtime.”



Mr. Green had looked up, eyes bloodshot, empty. “So what now? Do we hide?”



“Stick to the same line,” Partridge had said. “We’re doing all we can; let us get on with the job.”



The couple had exchanged a disbelieving glance. Tea had gone cold. The biscuits, stale. Partridge had promised a liaison officer and daily briefings.



He had driven back to Kent, to revisit the streets and gardens of his youth. Tanya Green had been eighteen, a former local beauty queen, found dead in the lighthouse that had shadowed his childhood.



Chapter One: Arrival



Partridge had arrived at Admiralty House on a damp, gray morning. Mist curled around the old Georgian building like smoke, softening the edges of the river and muting the cries of gulls. He had remembered the house as a boy, when the grounds had been tidy and the lighthouse active, a steadfast beacon cutting through storm and fog.



Now, it had grown wild. Ivy climbed the walls, shutters swung on rusted hinges, and the garden had become a tangle of nettles and brambles. The newcomers had arrived the day before, bringing laughter and boxes, furniture and optimism. They had not noticed the shadows in the hallways, the weight in the stairwells, or the faint smell of brine that clung to the wood.



Partridge had watched them from the edge of the path, unseen. He had memorized every face, every movement. The past pressed against him with a weight that was both familiar and suffocating.





Chapter Two: Memory and History



The blackness had crept over him as he walked through the gardens. Leaves crunched beneath wet boots; the damp smell of mushrooms rose from the soil. Shadows moved across the untended flowerbeds, nature reclaiming the place with ease.



The Admiralty Gardens had been foreboding in his childhood; now they were terrifying. Partridge had climbed the rusty ladders to the lighthouse tower, the metal flaking into his hands. At the top, the glass light encased the scene of Tanya Green’s murder. Her body had been discovered just days earlier, limbs arranged within a pentagram drawn in purple chalk. The precision, the ritual, had chilled him.



Forensics had scoured the scene: strangulation marks, drug traces, sawed limbs, traces of alcohol. Whoever had done this had planned it meticulously. Yet no familiar prints, no familiar DNA. The killer was unknown.



Partridge had staggered down the ladders, emptied his stomach behind the hedge, and then driven to the Green residence. He had asked the usual questions: friends, places visited, last messages, diaries, social networks. The answers had been fragments, pieces in a puzzle that didn’t yet make sense.



The lighthouse had not forgotten. It had witnessed fires, drownings, and disappearances, all cataloged in old logs and whispered among the locals. Alfred and Frederick Trimble had vanished one winter, leaving only footprints frozen in snow. Emily Trimble had kept the light burning during two world wars, her eyes haunted by the reflection of distant fires.



Tanya’s death had been another mark upon its history. Precise, deliberate, ritualistic. Partridge had felt it immediately upon entering the lighthouse that night. The rooms had smelled of old wood, sea, and something metallic beneath. Her parents’ grief had been raw and sharp, cutting through the formalities of police procedure.



He had walked the halls alone after they had left, tracing the memories embedded in the floorboards and railings. Every step was a story. Every crack in the plaster a whisper of lives long gone.

Chapter Three: The Greens’ Grief



Mr. and Mrs. Green had retreated to their home on the outskirts of Chatham, their world reduced to echoes of Tanya’s laughter and the relentless press. They had clung to each other, fragile and trembling, trying to reconstruct the life that had been shattered in a single night.



Partridge had visited daily, bearing news, support, and the careful shield of professionalism. He had listened to their stories of Tanya’s childhood, her school days, her dreams of studying art in London. He had watched them struggle with the press, with the intrusive questions, and with the impossible task of accepting that the past could not be rewritten.





Chapter Four: The Newcomers



Inside Admiralty House, the newcomers had begun to settle. A young couple, their laughter ringing through the hallways, furniture rearranged to suit modern tastes. They had hung bright curtains, unpacked books and pots, unaware of the shadows lingering in corners.



Partridge had observed them from a distance. He had recognized the danger in innocence, the way life carried on in defiance of history. He had remembered how Tanya had moved through the same spaces, unaware that someone or something had been watching.



At night, he had walked the grounds again, noting the wind through broken panes, the sound of the tide against the riverbank, and the faint echoes of footsteps that did not belong to the living.





Chapter Five: Shadows and Whispers



The lighthouse whispered to him in the darkness. He had imagined voices in the stairwell, figures gliding across the lamp room, the creak of the old pulley systems. Shadows stretched long across the walls, taking forms that vanished the moment he looked directly.



He had remembered the first discovery of Tanya, and the precise cruelty of her death. Every detail was etched into his mind, every pattern a key to understanding the past. Yet he had also known that some stories did not need solving—they demanded patience, observation, and respect.



The active case had passed to younger officers. Partridge had lingered, observing them. He saw their inexperience, their eagerness. They moved through the lighthouse as the Trimble family had moved decades before, unaware of the echoes they traced.



A new caretaker, unfamiliar with the tales of Emily Trimble and the vanished keepers, climbed the tower to inspect the light. The narrator in Partridge’s mind traced every motion: steps on the ladders, hands on the pulleys, each echoing a long-dead routine.



Even the daughter of a naval officer, wandering through the overgrown gardens, unknowingly retraced the paths of children who had played there long before. The lighthouse, patient and silent, watched it all.





Chapter Six: Convergence



Time had folded over itself. The past, Tanya’s murder, the Greens’ grief, and the newcomers’ lives all converged in the lighthouse. Partridge had seen the threads intersect, the way history shaped the present, and the way the present would inevitably leave its mark on history.



He had followed small clues, subtle signs—a misplaced book, a smell, a draft that shifted unexpectedly. The house spoke in quiet signals, guiding him, warning him, reminding him that the weight of the past was never truly gone.





Chapter Seven: Acceptance



By the end, Partridge had realized that the lighthouse was both a prison and a sanctuary. The past lingered, yes, but life continued, fragile and insistent. The newcomers carried on, unknowingly stepping through history as they claimed the rooms as their own.



The Greens had begun to find small moments of peace, moments in which Tanya’s absence was a shadow that did not entirely suffocate joy. And Partridge had stood at the window, watching the sun break across the river, feeling the patient pull of memory and time.



Forensics had returned some answers: a mixture of unknown male DNA, a trace that would eventually lead to the perpetrator. But Partridge had known the deeper story. This lighthouse, this place, had always been a theater for human tragedy and obsession. Tanya Green was another thread in a centuries-long pattern.



He had left the scene, watching the new officers take their place. The old ghosts were still there, quiet and waiting. The demolition, scheduled in a few months, would erase the structure—but not its memory.



And Partridge, standing one last time in the autumn sun, knew that some places never truly let go of the past.



The lighthouse remained. The past remained. And so did he, quietly, watching, remembering, and waiting.
