What Thirty Years of Teaching Taught Me

The learner matters more than the system, the authority, or the teacher’s ego.

22 September 2026 — Richard Jenkins · Teaching · Learning · Educational relationships

Teaching is one of society’s most important jobs. It is also undervalued, misunderstood, and exhausting. Teachers plan, deliver, check, adapt, repeat—and then begin again with the next group.

The most important person in education is the learner. That simple truth is forgotten by authorities and sometimes by teachers who begin to believe that the institution, the inspection, the curriculum, or their own authority matters most. A good teacher takes every reasonable opportunity to teach or help, while keeping the learner’s best interests at heart.

The Classroom Checklist

Every lesson carries its own running commentary:

  • WALT—We Are Learning Today—written on the board and explained? Check.
  • Handouts handed out? Check.
  • Students understand the task? Check.
  • Register taken? Check.
  • Walk around and answer questions? Check.
  • Change the rhythm before attention dissolves into boredom or daydreaming? Check.
  • Respond to mischievous behaviour without losing the lesson? Check.
  • Watch the time, add or remove a task, explain the homework? Check.
  • Check that the lesson objective was learned and appears in their books? Check.
  • Close the lesson—and prepare to do it all again? Check.

Not every learner moves at the same pace. Not every learner wants to be in the room. And learners absorb far more than the intended curriculum. They learn from our behaviour, from rumours racing around the school, from friendships and romances, and from the endless stream of information supplied by television, magazines, radio, phones, and social networks.

I learned that institutions can become games of status and self-protection. Some managers appeared threatened by initiative. Information offered in trust could later return sharpened into a weapon. In such systems, teachers and learners can both lose sight of why the school exists.

Students leave with no qualifications, qualifications that carry too little meaning, or qualifications chosen before they could reasonably know what their lives would require. We ask young people to make permanent choices at fourteen, although few of them will remain in the same occupation for forty years.

Students die. Staff die. Parents die. Families fracture. Somehow, the teacher has to guide learners through the mess of life while swimming through it too.

The Three Pillars

The best educational idea I developed over those thirty years was built on three pillars:

  1. Connection: feeling safe and feeling seen.
  2. Construction: building purpose and motivation.
  3. Conversation: developing real curiosity instead of blind acceptance.

1. Connection—Building a Link with Love

For learning to be truly effective, there must first be a genuine connection with the teacher. Our school memories rarely revolve around worksheets or examination criteria. They are about the teachers who inspired us, encouraged us, or cared enough to build a relationship. We remember the feeling of being seen more readily than the information we memorised.

As many colleagues have told me: “You have to love the students.” This does not mean sentimentality or blurred professional boundaries. It means holding an active commitment to their dignity, safety, and possibility.

A healthy, secure rapport is not a decorative bonus. When learners feel respected and recognised, they are more willing to participate, take intellectual risks, and allow curiosity to happen. Some students thrive independently, but most need what human beings have always needed: relationship, belonging, and attention.

Learning Begins Before School

Long before entering a classroom, children learn to communicate, move through the world, play, relate to others, and test cause and effect. Parents and carers support these developments through affection, repetition, responsiveness, and presence.

Connection often matters before content. Relationship becomes part of the curriculum.

When children experience severe neglect, isolation, or abuse, development and learning can be profoundly affected. No curriculum can simply erase the absence of care.

The Case of Genie

“Genie” is the pseudonym given to an American girl commonly described as a feral child. Born in 1957, she spent much of her childhood confined and almost entirely isolated by her abusive father. California authorities discovered her in 1970, when she was thirteen. She was severely malnourished, could barely walk, and had not acquired ordinary spoken language.

Her discovery drew intense interest from psychologists and linguists. Researchers hoped that helping her learn to communicate might also illuminate a difficult question: is there a critical period in childhood after which a first language can never be fully acquired? Genie learned vocabulary and some forms of communication, but continued to struggle profoundly with grammar and social development.

The research remains ethically troubling. The people caring for Genie were also studying her, funding disputes disrupted that care, and she later returned to inadequate foster placements. Older reporting placed her in state-supported care in California, but her present circumstances are private and have not been reliably confirmed in recent public reporting.

Her case cannot isolate love, language, trauma, nutrition, cognition, and human contact as though they were separate laboratory variables. It should not be reduced to a neat proof. It does, however, show the terrible developmental consequences that can accompany extreme deprivation—and reminds us that language and identity grow within relationships.

2. Construction—Building the Desire to Learn

Once connection exists, a teacher can help construct the motivation that drives active learning. Learners need to understand why something matters, how it connects to what they already know, and what they might be able to do with it.

Purpose cannot always be imposed. It has to be built with the learner. When students begin to want to learn, teaching becomes guidance rather than struggle.

3. Conversation—Building the Curiosity to Question

I had the chance to work with Reed Meister about a decade ago. His ideas about conversation and ethics changed my teaching practice. Conversation provides a non-violent way to explore possibilities.

Louder does not mean better.

How different this is from the conflict and confusion of contemporary politics and education debates, where people cling to broken dogma and speak mainly to drown out other ideas.

Conversation is where learning becomes transformative. Students shift from passive recipients to active co-creators of knowledge. Curiosity thrives in dialogue, not in silence or dogma.

We Need a Different Way

We cannot continue with a system that breaks learners and teachers and then blames them for the fractures. When students feel unseen and teachers are overwhelmed, curriculum reform alone cannot repair the deeper problem.

The fractures created in education can become fractures in society. Instead of a virtuous cycle of upward joy, we create a siphon of destruction. Some learners turn away from education altogether. Disengagement can become exclusion; exclusion can deepen vulnerability; anger can harden into rebellion or hate.

These outcomes are not inevitable, and they cannot be blamed on schools alone. But education should not contribute to them by repeatedly telling young people that they have failed.

Fail again. Fail better.

This is not simply a failure of individuals. It is a failure of a framework that ignores the human foundations of learning: belonging, attention, purpose, and trust.

If education does not place connection at its centre, we will continue to produce burnout, boredom, and the belief that school is something to survive rather than somewhere to grow. The joy of learning will disappear beneath administration and compliance.

Connection is not optional. It is the foundation of meaningful learning, healthy society, and honest debate. It is also one of our protections against extremists of every persuasion who demand that everyone think exactly as they do.
