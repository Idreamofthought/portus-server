Nerves Tuned to Failure

There is a particular kind of competence that never quite believes in itself — the practised teacher who still feels, walking into a familiar classroom, that today might be the day it's discovered they don't actually know what they're doing. This isn't false modesty. It's a nervous system that has been tuned, somewhere along the way, to treat failure as the more plausible outcome, regardless of the evidence sitting in the room.

Imposter syndrome gets described as a belief — “I secretly believe I'm a fraud” — but it behaves more like a reflex. It doesn't wait to be argued with. Before conscious thought has time to weigh the evidence (the years of experience, the results, the trust other people have already placed in you), the older circuitry has already flagged the situation as dangerous and started preparing an exit. The mistake is treating this as a debate you can win with facts, when it's closer to a startle response that facts don't reach.

Self-sabotage often follows the same wiring, just with the timeline reversed. If failure feels inevitable, there's a strange relief in bringing it about yourself — underpreparing, procrastinating, picking the fight before the rejection can pick you. A failure you engineered at least confirms you were right to be afraid, and confirmation, even of something painful, can feel more bearable than uncertainty. It's a way of trading a slow, open-ended anxiety for a short, familiar pain with a known shape.

What tends to loosen this pattern isn't reassurance — reassurance bounces off a reflex the same way facts do. It's exposure without the predicted catastrophe: doing the thing, imperfectly, and noticing that the room didn't collapse. Nerves tuned to failure get retuned slowly, through repetition, not through a single moment of being convinced. This is part of why competent people so often remain quietly afraid for years after they've stopped having any real reason to be — the tuning happened early, and updating it takes far longer than the achievements that should, logically, have settled the question.

The kinder framing is that this isn't a character flaw to be confessed and cured. It's closer to an old alarm system, installed for good enough reasons once, still firing on a threshold that no longer matches the actual risk. The work isn't to silence it through willpower. It's to keep giving it new, contradicting data — and to notice, eventually, when it starts firing a little less often.
