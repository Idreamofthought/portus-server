Belonging, Exclusion and the Institutions Between Us

Belonging is rarely granted directly, person to person. It's mediated — by a school that decides who counts as a promising student, a workplace that decides whose style of speaking reads as competence, a neighbourhood association that decides whose noise is community and whose noise is a nuisance. Institutions are the machinery through which “us” gets defined, quietly, procedurally, often without anyone in the room intending exclusion at all.

That's what makes exclusion durable. Overt prejudice is at least legible — it can be named, opposed, embarrassed out of a room. Institutional exclusion is different: it runs on criteria that sound neutral. Merit. Fit. Professionalism. Readiness. Each of these words does real work and also, quite often, encodes whose background made the criteria feel natural in the first place. The person excluded by “fit” rarely gets an explanation they can argue with, because there's nothing to point to except a feeling the institution has learned to trust.

The people who fall outside these criteria don't simply lose access to a resource. They lose the specific, irreplaceable experience of being expected — of walking into a room where your presence was already accounted for rather than something to be explained. Belonging, at its most basic, is not being liked. It's not having to justify your presence before you've done anything at all.

What complicates this further is that institutions are also, genuinely, how large numbers of strangers manage to cooperate at all. A hospital that only treated people its staff happened to like personally would be a catastrophe. Rules, procedures, and formal criteria exist because informal trust doesn't scale. The problem isn't that institutions sort people — some sorting is unavoidable, even necessary. The problem is when the sorting criteria stop being examined, when “this is how we've always done it” becomes indistinguishable from “this is fair,” and when the people doing the sorting are never the people affected by it.

This is, in the end, a design question as much as a moral one. Institutions that build in review — that ask, periodically and honestly, who is quietly being kept out by rules that were never meant to exclude anyone — tend to widen belonging without collapsing into chaos. Institutions that treat their own criteria as beyond question tend to calcify, mistaking the shape of their past membership for the definition of merit itself. Between us, always, sits some structure deciding who counts. The only real choice is whether that structure is willing to look at itself.
