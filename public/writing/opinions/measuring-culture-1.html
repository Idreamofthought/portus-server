Measuring Culture

2026-07-28 — Culture · Culture & society — Tags: culture, society, measurement


1. What culture is and why it exists



Culture is not a measure of superiority or advancement. It arises naturally from human activities, exchanges, and interactions. Many elements of culture — art, music, symbols, rituals — go back tens of thousands of years. Cave art, petroglyphs, counting marks on bones, burials, and early rituals all show that humans (and even Neanderthals) created cultural expressions long before writing existed.



2. Prehistoric foundations of culture



Archaeological evidence shows:




  • Cave art as early as 70,000 BCE (Blombos Cave)


  • Cave paintings around 45,500 years ago


  • Neanderthal musical instruments around 60,000 years ago


  • Paleolithic flutes around 35,000 years ago



From these early practices emerged ideas that shaped humanity: language, mathematics, religion, symbolism, and storytelling.



Compare that to writing, or even numbers. The earliest finds we have showing that people were probably counting are things like the Ishango Bone, (from around 20 thousand years ago but debated) or the much older Lebombo bone, from around 40 thousand years ago, but debated) and tally marks on cave walls. Culture predates all of this.



3. Culture as local and global



Cultural practices could be local or widespread. Cave art, burial rituals, and trade networks appear across the world. Some practices were adopted, others abandoned, and many changed due to:




  • invasions


  • migrations


  • climate shifts


  • technological improvements



Shared styles may explain similarities between sites thousands of years old, though we cannot assume a single cultural thread linking them all.



4. Geography, movement, and cultural exchange



Culture continually shifts. Trade by river, sea, or land routes spread ideas and habits. Geographical barriers — mountains, seas, valleys — created isolation, distinct languages, and unique cultural traits. Technological improvements in sailing and transport increased cultural exchange.



Today, the internet accelerates cultural change daily, making culture more fluid than ever.



5. Understanding other cultures — and stereotypes



We only truly know the cultures we are exposed to. When encountering unfamiliar cultures, they may seem strange, frightening, or incomprehensible. This often leads to mockery or stereotypes.



Examples:




  • French people “mock” English eating habits.


  • English people “mock” French eating habits.



Yet stereotypes rarely match reality: few French people eat snails or frogs daily, and English roast beef is no longer a universal Sunday tradition.



6. Culture in personal experience



Even within one lifetime, culture changes. Practices once common become rare; traditions fade or transform. What was taboo becomes accepted or even normal. Society still maintains social and legal norms, but expectations evolve rapidly.



7. Religion as an example of cultural change



In the UK, religious identity has shifted dramatically:




  • In 1983: 66% Christian, 31% no religion


  • In 2018: 38% Christian, 52% non‑religious



Church attendance has fallen by hundreds of thousands. Where most people once attended church weekly, now most do not.



8. Cultural traces that remain



Despite change, culture leaves marks everywhere:




  • French day names come from Roman gods.


  • English day names come from Viking gods.


  • Christianity adapted earlier Celtic festivals.


  • Words like Friday (Frigg/Frija) and Easter (possibly Eostre) preserve ancient mythologies.



9. The difficulty of defining culture



Culture is vast, shifting, and complex. As soon as we try to measure or capture it, it changes. We stand in the present, looking back at what was lost, what survived, and what transformed — like King Canute trying to command the tide.



Some have tried to measure culture, Geerte Hofstede, Erin Meyers among others. but even their attempts needed updates and are probably outdated even now.
