First They Came for the Words

Language, erasure, and the politics of making people disappear from public life.

22 September 2026 — Richard Jenkins · Politics & Society — Sources checked at publication; rapidly evolving claims may change.

Cécile Alduy, a Stanford professor and researcher in discourse analysis, writes in D’abord ils sont venus chercher les mots — literally, First They Came for the Words — that the Trump administration has quietly removed or discouraged certain terms in federal documents, scientific programmes, grant criteria, and public communication. Drawing on a multi-year analysis of United States government websites and administrative language, she describes hundreds of words associated with gender, race, climate, vaccines, and reproductive rights disappearing from official use.

There is no single official “banned words list”. Authoritarian habits rarely arrive as a neat PDF. Instead, Alduy documents a pattern of silent erasure. Terms including gender, LGBT, racism, climate change, autism, abortion, and vaccine have been removed, discouraged, or used to flag material for review across parts of the federal government. Reporting and public-interest monitoring have documented comparable restrictions, including instructions affecting federal health research and communication.

This linguistic purge has consequences beyond vocabulary. Research that depends on targeted language can be delayed, defunded, altered, or halted. Alduy’s thesis can be expressed brutally simply:

Erase the word, and you erase the person.

Freedom of speech is protected by the First Amendment — a guarantee every president swears to uphold. Yet the systematic removal of politically inconvenient vocabulary from public institutions looks suspiciously like the behaviour of someone auditioning for the role of tin-pot strongman.

Trump’s tactic of throwing magical distraction powder into the sky — dazzling headlines, attacks on the media, and sudden controversies — while cynical shenanigans continue behind the smoke is an old trick, as ancient as the hills and just as constant. Media researchers have repeatedly examined how spectacle and outrage can overwhelm sustained scrutiny of policy.

Trump has also floated direct cash payments with an explicit electoral frame. In September 2026, he promised a $5,000 “Trump dividend” for American adults if Republicans retained control of Congress. Associated Press and Reuters reported that the proposal would require congressional approval, could cost more than a trillion dollars, and arrived without a detailed funding mechanism.

Meanwhile, politically connected companies continue to seek favourable regulatory and land-use decisions. Claims about any particular company, permit, or parcel of supposedly protected land must be checked case by case against official state and federal records. The broader question remains: who receives permission, who bears the environmental cost, and whose objections are heard?

And while Trump plasters the United States flag across territories that are not American and proclaims himself a genius, he clings to the childish belief that ignoring problems makes them disappear. Climate change, unfortunately, does not vanish on command. Friends of mine — loyal Trump supporters — watched their ranch burn down, their life’s work swallowed by flames. One fire cannot by itself be attributed to climate change, but the scientific evidence is clear that human-caused warming is increasing the conditions that make many wildfires more likely and destructive. In Trump’s broken world, the climate crisis feeding those risks is treated as though it does not exist.
