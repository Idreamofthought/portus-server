Fungi: The Underground Conversation

A living conversation has been running beneath our feet since long before we had words for wonder.

22 September 2026 — Richard Jenkins · Fungi · Mycorrhiza · Ecology

I can still remember the spark in our teacher’s eyes back in 1988, when she spoke of mycorrhiza as if revealing a secret kingdom. We nodded along, thinking we understood. To us, fungi were simple partners: taking a little carbon from the plant, giving back a few nutrients, and quietly decomposing the roots when life was done. A tidy exchange, a footnote in biology.

We had no idea we were standing on top of a living circuitry.

Beneath every woodland path, beneath every meadow, beneath the quiet soil of our gardens, fungi weave networks — vast, shifting webs that can link plants into communities. Experiments have found defence signals passing between some plants through common mycorrhizal networks, prompting neighbours to change their chemistry before an attack reaches them. The popular image of trees freely “talking” across every forest goes beyond what the evidence can yet prove, but the measured exchanges are remarkable enough.

Humans built the internet and puffed out their chests, proud of their cables and servers, unaware that nature had already evolved a living internet millions of years earlier — one that pulsed with sunlight, soil, and survival.

We wandered through forests with guidebooks, reciting the old rule: “If you don’t know it, don’t touch it.” Some avoided agarics entirely, terrified of the elegant killers hiding among the amanitas. Mushrooms and humans have walked side by side for ages, and Terence McKenna’s “Stoned Ape” hypothesis still drifts through conversations — the idea that hallucinogenic mushrooms nudged our minds open. A colourful speculation, but not an accepted scientific explanation of human evolution.

Yet the real story — the one still unfolding — is far stranger and far more beautiful.

The New Underground: What Science Has Revealed

Researchers increasingly describe fungal networks not as static webs but as dynamic systems shaped by climate, soil, host plants, disease, and geography. A broad survey published in 2026 found a shift towards arbuscular-mycorrhizal tree dominance across the Japanese archipelago over fifteen years, associated with warming and spreading tree disease. The forest floor is not a constant; it is a tide.

Plants under heat, drought, or saline stress can benefit from fungal partners. Across controlled studies, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi have improved nutrient access, altered stress responses, and helped some plants perform better under harsh conditions. They are not a universal shield — the outcome depends on the plant, fungus, soil, and stress — but they can act like biological shock absorbers.

And the soil itself — that dark, granular universe — is being quietly redesigned. Experiments show arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi reshaping the microbial neighbourhood around roots, altering bacterial communities and phosphorus cycling. Even in saline and soda-saline soils, these relationships can make conditions more favourable for plant growth.

But the most astonishing discoveries lie in the realm of communication.

Plants and fungi speak in molecular languages we never imagined. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi release signals known as Myc factors, including lipochitooligosaccharides and short-chain chitin molecules. Plants detect them through specialised receptor systems. It is not merely crude barter; recognition and signalling begin before colonisation is established.

Even more extraordinary, fungi can send small RNA messages across the boundary between kingdoms. Research published in 2025 and 2026 found small RNAs from Rhizophagus irregularis entering plant cells and silencing particular plant genes in ways that promote root colonisation. Cross-kingdom whispering is no longer just metaphor.

Then there are extracellular vesicles — microscopic membrane-bound packages capable of carrying biological cargo. Researchers have observed the cellular machinery and membrane traffic that make them plausible messengers at the mycorrhizal interface, but their precise role in this symbiosis remains an active question rather than a finished discovery.

This is not the simple partnership we learned in school.
It is a conversation — ancient, intricate, and alive.

Closing Atmosphere

So when we walk through a forest in autumn, kicking through leaves and scanning for chanterelles or ceps, we are not just foraging. We are stepping across a vast, humming network that predates us, exceeds our understanding, and quietly sustains the world we depend on.

Humans created the internet.
Fungi created the conversation.

And it has been running beneath our feet since long before we had words for wonder.
