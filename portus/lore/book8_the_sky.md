# The Sky

## The Reckoning Bone

Before Portus had towers, the shore-keepers carried a dark bone scored with
small cuts. Each cut was a night that had returned. Each gap between cuts was
a promise that it would return again.

The First Reader watched the moon empty and grow full. She counted the blood
months of her people, the tides, and the seven wandering lights: the Sun, the
Moon, and the five lights that moved against the fixed stars. When the count
became too large to hold in memory, she gave the bone to the water and asked
it to remember.

The water returned it marked in three paths. One path held the odd steps, one
held the doubled steps, and one held the numbers that could be divided among
many hands. The shore-keepers called the whole count the Long Measure. Later
readers called it sixty.

They did not believe the bone commanded the sky. They believed it taught the
eye to notice return: a moon after a moon, a season after a season, a world
made legible by what came back.

When the sea swallowed the old shore, the bone was carried inland. Portus
still keeps its image beneath the Observatory, not as proof of a single first
calendar, but as a reminder that time begins when someone chooses to count
what matters.
